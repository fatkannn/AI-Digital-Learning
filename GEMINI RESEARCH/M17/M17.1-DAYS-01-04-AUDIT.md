## Day 1 — How Computers Work

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 1 — Digital Foundations. **Sub:** `computer`.
- **Objectives:** describe what CPU/RAM/storage/GPU each do and why the difference matters;
  explain why a program must load into RAM before running; predict which component bottlenecks a
  given workload.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, fetch-decode-execute loop, four components) → Core
  material (20m, CPU/RAM/storage speed-and-persistence tradeoff, GPU parallelism and its link to
  neural-network matrix multiplication) → Hands-on (15m, system monitor / Task Manager / Activity
  Monitor / htop, watch RAM behavior across browser tabs) → Practice (10m, name the bottleneck for
  three real tasks) → Reflection (5m, explain to a non-technical colleague why more RAM sometimes
  does nothing).
- **Example:** a compute-bound video render misdiagnosed as needing more RAM — the diagnostic
  habit ("read the meters first") is the transferable skill.
- **Practice/Challenge:** profile your own machine; predict, before Day 20, which spec will
  decide whether a local AI model runs at all.
- **Recap (Indonesian):** correctly restates the CPU/RAM/storage/GPU model and the "lemot ≠
  kurang RAM" misconception.
- **Resources (3, all `verified: true`):** Khan Academy "Computers and the Internet" (general
  intro), Harvard CS50x (official, self-refreshing URL), CS50's Understanding Technology (labeled
  "(arsip)" — correctly disclosed as retired 30 June 2024, kept for still-valid hardware/network
  concepts).
- **Quiz (6 questions):** CPU-reads-from-RAM mechanism; RAM volatility; why GPUs suit AI
  (thousands of parallel cores); two bottleneck-diagnosis scenarios (compute-bound vs.
  memory/swap-bound); short-answer on the RAM-vs-storage distinction.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct throughout. The fetch-decode-execute framing, RAM volatility, and
   GPU-as-parallel-matrix-math explanation all hold up against how these concepts are taught in
   introductory CS material.
2. **Clarity** — strong. No unexplained jargon; "bottleneck" and "compute-bound" are defined in
   use via the worked example.
3. **Depth** — appropriate for Day 1 of 28. Doesn't overreach into cache hierarchies or
   instruction pipelining, which would be premature here.
4. **Practicality** — high. The hands-on task uses the learner's own machine; the practice task
   ties directly to real workloads (video editing, local models, spreadsheets).
5. **Examples** — the video-render example is concrete and correctly diagnosed; it models the
   actual reasoning skill (read the meters, don't guess).
6. **Active learning** — yes: system-monitor observation, own-machine profiling, and a written
   prediction that pays off three weeks later on Day 20. This forward-reference is a genuine
   strength, not filler.
7. **Misconceptions** — explicitly addressed ("lemot berarti kurang RAM" in the recap).
8. **Current relevance** — **one gap identified, see Research Findings below.**
9. **Resource quality** — good. All three resources are `verified: true`, appropriately leveled,
   and the archived-course disclosure is handled honestly rather than hidden.
10. **Assessment alignment** — all three objectives are directly tested: q1 tests
    RAM-before-execution; q3 tests GPU/AI rationale; q4/q5 test bottleneck prediction from
    two different symptom patterns (compute-bound and swap-bound), which is a stronger test of the
    prediction objective than a single scenario would be.

### Research Findings

The lesson's hardware model — CPU, RAM, storage, GPU — omits a category that has become
mainstream in exactly the two years since this project's last general hardware review: the
**NPU (Neural Processing Unit)**, a dedicated AI-inference chip now shipping in most new Windows
laptops (Copilot+ PCs) and already present in Apple Silicon Macs (Neural Engine) and recent phones.

Opened directly (not from search snippets):

- **Microsoft Learn, "Copilot+ PCs developer guide"**
  (`https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/ai/npu-devices/`, official Microsoft, updated
  2026-07-15). Confirms: an NPU is a dedicated chip "designed specifically to execute the deep
  learning math operations that make up AI models," rated in TOPS (trillions of operations per
  second, 40+ TOPS for Copilot+ certification), more power-efficient than CPU or GPU for AI
  workloads, and — directly relevant to this day's own hands-on activity — **Windows Task Manager
  now shows a dedicated NPU column alongside CPU, Memory, GPU, and Disk.** The page also confirms
  this is not Windows-only in spirit: NPUs from Qualcomm, Intel, and AMD are all covered, and the
  underlying hardware category (a dedicated low-power AI accelerator distinct from the CPU and
  GPU) is the same one Apple ships as the "Neural Engine" in every Apple Silicon Mac since 2020 —
  though the Apple-specific primary source could not be successfully opened this session (the
  fetch returned no usable body content), so that specific claim is stated from general knowledge,
  not independently source-confirmed here, and is flagged as such.
- **Microsoft Support, "All about neural processing units (NPUs)"**
  (`https://support.microsoft.com/windows/all-about-neural-processing-units-npus-e77a5637-7705-4915-96c8-0c6a975f9db4`,
  official Microsoft). Corroborates: NPUs "use less power and are far more efficient at AI tasks
  than a CPU or GPU" and "free up the CPU and GPU for other tasks."

Both sources are **A — official/primary**.

### Enhancement Opportunities

Add a short mention of the NPU as a fifth hardware category increasingly relevant to exactly the
question this day already asks the learner to reason about ("which spec decides whether local AI
works"). This is not a correction — the existing CPU/GPU framing is accurate for the general case
and for anyone on older hardware — but it is a real, dated gap: a learner who runs the day's own
Task Manager hands-on activity on a 2024+ Windows laptop or Apple Silicon Mac will now see an NPU
column the lesson gives them no framework for.

### Decision

**ENHANCE**

### Priority

**P2** — useful, not urgent. The four-component model remains correct and pedagogically sufficient
on its own; this is a modern-context enrichment, not a fix to something wrong.

### Evidence

See Research Findings — two official Microsoft sources (source quality A), both opened directly
this session.

### Implementation Notes (not applied this session)

1. **What exists now:** CPU/RAM/storage/GPU as the complete hardware model.
2. **What's missing:** any mention of the NPU, now a mainstream AI-specific accelerator.
3. **What the research contributes:** confirmation that this is a real, named, official hardware
   category (not a marketing term) with a defined role distinct from CPU and GPU, and that it is
   now directly observable via the same Task Manager tool the lesson already has the learner open.
4. **Why the learner needs it:** the day's own challenge asks the learner to predict which spec
   decides whether local AI works — for a growing share of real 2025–2026 hardware, the accurate
   answer now involves the NPU, not only the GPU.
5. **Objective supported:** "Predict which component is the bottleneck for a given workload"
   (objective 3) and indirectly the GPU/AI-relevance explanation (objective 1).
6. **Where it would fit:** one or two sentences appended to the end of the Core material segment's
   GPU paragraph (not a new segment), plus an optional one-line note in the Hands-on segment
   telling learners with an NPU-equipped machine to also check that meter.
7. **Time impact:** ~1 additional minute of reading, not a new timed segment — within the existing
   20-minute Core material budget.
8. **Cognitive load:** low — it's an additional label on an existing "which component does the
   work" mental model the learner is already building, not a new concept type.
9. **Could something be shortened instead?** No — nothing existing is redundant or removable to
   make room; the addition is small enough not to require trading anything out.
10. **Post-test impact:** none proposed. The existing GPU-focused quiz questions (q3, q4, q5)
    remain valid without change; a new NPU-specific question is not necessary for a P2 enrichment.
11. **New resource required?** No — the two sources above are sufficient citations if this is ever
    implemented; no new curriculum resource entry is proposed.
12. **New activity required?** No — folds into the existing hands-on system-monitor activity.

### Estimated Time Impact

Negligible (~1 minute of additional reading; no new segment, no change to the 60-minute total).

### Assessment Impact

None proposed. No quiz question requires addition or modification for this enhancement to be
coherent.

### Resource Impact

None. No new resource entry proposed; the two sources cited are evidence for the enhancement's
justification, not additions to the day's resource list.

## Day 2 — Operating Systems

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 1. **Sub:** `computer`.
- **Objectives:** explain what an OS manages; distinguish process from program and file path from
  file; use the terminal for basic navigation with confidence about what each command does.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (OS as referee between hardware and applications; process
  management, memory management, file system) → Core material (program vs. process, memory
  isolation as a security boundary, read/write/execute permissions, terminal as a text interface
  to the same OS services the GUI uses) → Hands-on (pwd/ls/cd/mkdir/cat, create a folder and file)
  → Practice (list processes over 200MB, note which were deliberately started) → Reflection (why
  process memory isolation matters).
- **Example:** the classic "file not found although it's on the desktop" bug, correctly diagnosed
  as a working-directory/relative-path issue.
- **Recap:** accurate; correctly frames the terminal as "not a different computer, not a
  more-powerful mode" — the risk is the lack of confirmation/undo, not extra privilege.
- **Resources (2, both `verified: true`):** freeCodeCamp "Command Line Crash Course" (specific,
  practical); Microsoft Learn training catalogue root (`https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/`).
- **Quiz (6 questions):** program-vs-process distinction; OS mediates disk I/O; working-directory
  cause of the file-not-found bug; permission-check-first troubleshooting order; process memory
  isolation; short-answer on OS responsibilities.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct. Process/program distinction, memory isolation, and permission model are
   all standard and correctly stated.
2. **Clarity** — strong; the "referee" framing for the OS and "not a different computer, not extra
   power" framing for the terminal are both effective beginner analogies already in place.
3. **Depth** — appropriate; doesn't wander into kernel/userspace internals or scheduling
   algorithms, which would be premature.
4. **Practicality** — high; the terminal exercise is hands-on and immediately transferable (the
   lesson explicitly ties it forward to Git, Python environments, Ollama, self-hosted n8n).
5. **Examples** — the file-not-found example is a genuinely common real bug, well chosen.
6. **Active learning** — yes, real terminal use with a concrete file-creation task.
7. **Misconceptions** — addressed directly (terminal ≠ more powerful mode).
8. **Current relevance** — no material gap found. Process/permission/terminal concepts are stable
   and not time-sensitive; no research was pursued here beyond confirming there was nothing to
   look for, per the instruction not to research for its own sake.
9. **Resource quality** — freeCodeCamp resource is specific and well-matched. The Microsoft Learn

10. **Assessment alignment** — good; all three objectives are tested, including a realistic

## Day 3 — Files, Data & Storage

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 1. **Sub:** `computer`.
- **Objectives:** explain file format vs. file extension; choose an appropriate format for text,
  tabular, structured, and media data; describe encoding and why it breaks things.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (file = named byte sequence; extension is a hint, not a
  guarantee) → Core material (text vs. binary formats; UTF-8 as the modern default; CSV/JSON/
  Markdown singled out as the three formats that matter most for this curriculum's later phases)
  → Hands-on (create/edit a CSV, observe what a spreadsheet app changes on save) → Practice
  (write the same records as JSON, compare parseability vs. human scannability) → Reflection (why
  unquoted commas break CSV).
- **Example:** an LMS import breaking because a name field ("Nurhaliza, Siti") contained an
  unquoted comma — correctly diagnosed as a structural delimiter problem, not a cosmetic one.
- **Recap:** accurate; correctly reframes "garbled Indonesian characters" as an encoding mismatch,
  not a font problem, and correctly notes that renaming a file's extension does not change its
  format.
- **Resources (2, both `verified: true`):** MDN "JSON — Working with structured data"; Python
  official docs on reading/writing files (with encoding handling).
- **Quiz (6 questions):** extension-vs-content; encoding-not-font misconception; JSON vs. CSV for
  nested data; the unquoted-comma CSV failure mode; UTF-8 as the correct default; short-answer on
  text vs. binary format readability.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct throughout; the file/format/extension distinction, encoding explanation,
   and CSV/JSON tradeoffs are all standard and correctly stated.
2. **Clarity** — strong; "a file is a named sequence of bytes, nothing more" is an effective,
   precise opening frame.
3. **Depth** — appropriately scoped. The lesson deliberately limits itself to three formats
   (CSV, JSON, Markdown) rather than surveying every serialization format, with an explicit
   rationale ("three formats carry disproportionate weight" for this curriculum's later phases) —
   this is a considered scope decision, not an omission.
4. **Practicality** — high; the CSV-editing hands-on directly demonstrates real spreadsheet
   round-trip quirks (quoting, separators, line endings).
5. **Examples** — the LMS-import comma bug is a realistic, well-chosen failure mode directly tied
   to the learner's stated career context (learning & development).
6. **Active learning** — yes; hand-converting the same data between CSV and JSON is a genuine
   comparative exercise, not passive reading.
7. **Misconceptions** — addressed directly (encoding vs. font; extension vs. format).
8. **Current relevance** — no gap found. UTF-8-as-default, CSV/JSON as the dominant interchange
   formats, and the encoding-mismatch failure mode are all stable facts, not time-sensitive ones.
9. **Resource quality** — both resources are specific, official (MDN, Python Software Foundation),
   and directly on-topic. No catalog-root or breadth problem here.
10. **Assessment alignment** — strong; every objective is tested, including a scenario question
    (the CSV-splitting bug) that mirrors the day's own worked example rather than testing an
    unrelated fact.

## Day 4 — Internet Fundamentals

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 1. **Sub:** `network`.
- **Objectives:** trace what happens between typing a URL and seeing a page; explain IP/DNS/HTTP
  in terms of the job each does; read a URL and identify its parts.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (the internet as many networks agreeing on shared protocols;
  IP/DNS/HTTP as the three that matter here) → Core material (IP addressing, DNS as a directory,
  HTTP request/response with methods and status codes, HTTPS = HTTP + TLS, "padlock means private,
  not safe") → Hands-on (browser DevTools Network tab, find a non-200 request) → Practice
  (label the parts of a real URL; predict the effect of adding a query string) → Reflection (why
  status codes exist at all).
- **Example:** an "the API is broken" complaint correctly re-diagnosed via a 401 status as an
  authentication problem (likely an expired token), not a broken API — modeling the same
  evidence-before-conclusion habit as Day 1's example.
- **Recap:** accurate; correctly separates "HTTPS means private" from "HTTPS means trustworthy,"
  and correctly notes that "the internet is down" is often actually a DNS failure.
- **Resources (2, both `verified: true`):** Cloudflare Learning Center "What is DNS?"; MDN HTTP
  overview and status codes.
- **Quiz (6 questions):** DNS's job (vs. TLS/DHCP/CDN distractors); 401 interpretation; the
  HTTPS-padlock-does-not-mean-trustworthy misconception; correct client behavior on 429
  (backoff, not retry-harder); URL host identification; short-answer on why status codes aid
  debugging.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct throughout; IP/DNS/HTTP role separation, the status-code semantics used
   (200/301/401/403/404/429/500), and the HTTPS-encrypts-but-doesn't-vouch-for-trust distinction
   are all standard and correctly stated.
2. **Clarity** — strong; the DNS-as-directory and HTTP-as-request/response framings are clear and
   the practice task (label scheme/host/path on a real URL) is concrete.
3. **Depth** — appropriately scoped to IP/DNS/HTTP; does not attempt to cover TCP handshakes,
   TLS internals, or HTTP/2 framing, which would be premature for a Day 4 foundations lesson.
4. **Practicality** — high; the DevTools Network-tab exercise uses a tool the learner will return
   to constantly, and the status-code triage table (practice segment) is directly reusable.
5. **Examples** — the 401-misdiagnosed-as-"API is broken" example is realistic and models good
   diagnostic reasoning, paralleling Day 1's bottleneck example.
6. **Active learning** — yes; real DevTools inspection and URL-parsing practice.
7. **Misconceptions** — addressed directly (padlock ≠ trustworthy; "internet down" ≠ always a
   real outage).
8. **Current relevance** — no material gap found. IP/DNS/HTTP/HTTPS fundamentals and the listed
   status codes are stable, foundational, and not meaningfully different in 2026 than when
   written. HTTP/2 or HTTP/3 wire-level differences were considered and judged out of scope: they
   don't change what this day's objectives ask the learner to do (trace the request lifecycle,
   explain the job of each protocol layer, read a URL), so adding them would be adding interesting
   trivia rather than capability, which the task's own critical question rules out.
9. **Resource quality** — both resources (Cloudflare Learning Center, MDN) are specific,
   authoritative, and correctly scoped to the day's content.
10. **Assessment alignment** — strong; all three objectives are tested, and the 429-backoff
    question tests applied judgment rather than definition recall.
