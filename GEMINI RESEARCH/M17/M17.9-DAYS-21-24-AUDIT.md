## Day 21 — Evaluating AI Systems

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 6 — Trust: Evaluation and Security. **Sub:** `eval`.
- **Objectives:** turn a vague quality goal into measurable success criteria; choose between
  code-based, human, and LLM-based grading; separate a retrieval failure from a generation
  failure in a pipeline.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, what an eval is and why regression is the reason it
  exists) → Core material (20m, success criteria before metrics; the three grading methods in
  cost/reliability order; component-level evaluation for pipelines, citing Ragas' retrieval vs.
  generation metric split; volume-over-polish and real-failure-sourced eval sets) → Hands-on
  (15m, build a ten-item mental eval against this app's own short-answer grader) → Practice
  (10m, write three success criteria and assign a grading method to each) → Reflection (5m,
  which criterion needed a human, and could it be reduced to a rule).
- **Example:** an unvalidated LLM-judge that systematically rewarded fluent-but-incomplete
  summaries — correctly illustrates why judge validation against human labels matters.
- **Practice/Challenge:** design the eval that would produce this very app's own documented
  1.8% off-topic-acceptance figure (L-10 in the state file) before reading how it was actually
  produced — a strong, self-referential capstone task.
- **Resources (2, both `verified: true`):** Anthropic's official eval-building docs, Ragas'
  metrics catalog.
- **Quiz (6 questions):** regression detection as the core value of fixed test sets; identifying
  a usable success criterion; LLM-judge validation requirement; component-level diagnosis for a
  RAG failure; matching a JSON-schema check to code-based grading; short-answer on why fixed
  sets beat impression-based judging.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct throughout; the code/human/LLM-judge ordering, the
   Ragas-sourced retrieval/generation metric split, and the "validate the judge" caution all
   match current, standard evaluation practice.
2. **Clarity** — strong; "success criteria" is defined by contrast with vague placeholders
   ("accurate," "high quality") rather than by abstract definition.
3. **Depth** — appropriate for Day 21 of 28, placed correctly after RAG (Day 19) and tool-calling
   (Day 20), both of which it references for grounding.
4. **Practicality** — high; the hands-on activity uses the learner's own experience of this
   app's grader rather than an invented dataset.
5. **Examples** — the judge-that-needed-judging example is concrete and models the actual
   failure mode (a plausible-looking score hiding real disagreement with human raters).
6. **Active learning** — yes: mental ten-case eval, a three-criterion writing task, and a
   challenge that asks the learner to design an eval before seeing this project's own.
7. **Misconceptions** — explicitly addressed (an LLM judge is not a neutral objective scorer).
8. **Current relevance** — no gap found; see Research Findings.
9. **Resource quality** — both resources verified, correctly scoped, and the Ragas resource's
   own summary already discloses it's a third-party library, not a standard, avoiding overclaim.
10. **Assessment alignment** — all three objectives are directly tested (q2 → criteria writing,
    q3/q5 → grading-method selection and judge validation, q4 → component-level diagnosis). No
    orphaned objective, no untaught-but-assessed concept.

## Day 22 — AI Security: Injection, Data and Keys

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 6 — Trust. **Sub:** `ai-security`.
- **Objectives:** explain prompt injection as instructions and data sharing one channel; trace
  where secrets and personal data can leak from an AI application; apply least privilege and
  human approval to a system that can take actions.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, no code/data separation in an LLM context; OWASP's
  LLM Top 10 places prompt injection #1 in every edition) → Core material (20m, direct vs.
  indirect injection and why there is no known reliable prompt-level cure; defenses that live
  outside the prompt — delimiters, least privilege, human approval, iteration/spend caps,
  logging; improper output handling as the mirror-image risk; data/retention decisions; secrets
  handling per Day 5's rule; NIST AI RMF as shared vocabulary) → Hands-on (15m, self-audit of
  what's been pasted into an assistant recently, and checking the assistant's actual retention
  setting) → Practice (10m, threat-model an assistant with at least one attack requiring no
  application access) → Reflection (5m, which single control would you keep).
- **Example:** an invoice/PDF with hidden white-on-white text instructing the assistant to
  forward emails — correctly illustrates indirect injection and that capability, not phrasing,
  bounds the damage.
- **Practice/Challenge:** least-privilege tool design exercise; a Day-19-callback challenge
  (attacker adds one document to a RAG index) that asks the learner to notice the
  prompt-wording-vs-architecture ratio of their own fix.
- **Resources (2, both `verified: true`):** OWASP GenAI LLM Top 10 (2026 edition), NIST AI Risk
  Management Framework.
- **Quiz (6 questions):** why injection is structurally hard to fix; distinguishing direct from
  indirect injection; that HTTPS does not mitigate injection; improper output handling as a
  classic-injection-vector risk; where an API key belongs; short-answer explaining injection to
  a colleague.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct and current in its mechanisms (no channel separation, direct vs.
   indirect injection, capability-not-phrasing framing). See Research Findings for the one claim
   checked externally.
2. **Clarity** — strong; each defense is named and given a one-line reason, not left as an
   unexplained list.
3. **Depth** — appropriate; correctly declines to promise a prompt-level fix, which would be
   the actual beginner misconception to avoid reinforcing.
4. **Practicality** — high; the retention-setting check is something almost no learner has
   actually done, and the lesson says so.
5. **Examples** — the hidden-instruction invoice example is realistic and widely-cited-shaped
   (matches the general pattern of real indirect-injection incidents) without inventing a fake
   citation for it.
6. **Active learning** — yes: personal audit, threat-modeling with a mandatory
   no-application-access attack, and a RAG-specific challenge that quantifies the
   prompt-vs-architecture ratio of the learner's own fix.
7. **Misconceptions** — explicitly and strongly addressed: "there is no known prompt wording
   that reliably makes a model ignore instructions embedded in its context" is stated as fact,
   not softened.
8. **Current relevance** — **one claim checked externally, see Research Findings; not
   contradicted, not independently reconfirmed for the newest edition specifically.**
9. **Resource quality** — both resources verified and current: the OWASP resource's own
   `hubungan` field already discloses the 2026 edition published August 2026 and that the 2025
   edition remains available at the same site; the NIST resource's own field already discloses
   AI RMF 1.0 is under revision. Both caveats predate this session (M9) and remain accurate.
10. **Assessment alignment** — all three objectives directly tested (q1/q2 → injection concept
    and direct/indirect distinction, q4 → output-handling leak vector supporting the
    secrets/data-leak objective, q5 → key placement, q3 → the HTTPS-is-not-injection-mitigation
    misconception). No gap.

  **FACT confirmed:** as fetched this session, this hub page serves the **2025** edition's full

**RECOMMENDATION (not applied):** none. The lesson's claim as worded — "has been number one in

## Day 23 — AI in Instructional Design

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 7 — Applied AI for Learning. **Sub:** `elearning`.
- **Objectives:** decide which parts of a learning-design workflow AI should and should not own;
  use AI for first drafts while keeping a human-owned source of truth; redesign an assessment a
  language model has made trivial.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, generative AI doesn't change what makes learning
  work, only production cost — and cheap production removes the constraint that used to force
  prioritisation) → Core material (20m, mapping AI onto the workflow phase by phase; the
  human-owned-source-of-truth/model-drafts/human-review/traceability pattern from Day 15;
  grounding beats instructing; assessment-integrity redesign over detection; policy as part of
  design, citing UNESCO) → Hands-on (15m, generate and score five distractors against three
  survival rules) → Practice (10m, redesign a chatbot-completable assessment) → Reflection (5m,
  what was kept vs. discarded from the model's output, as a description of the learner's own
  expertise).
- **Example:** a compliance-refresher scenario writer that improves sharply once grounded in the
  actual policy document rather than a described one.
- **Practice/Challenge:** a review-gate design task; an honest before/after estimate of whether
  AI tools would have made a past module better or only faster.
- **Resources (2):** UNESCO's generative-AI-in-education guidance (`verified: true`); the full
  UNESDOC PDF version (`verified: false`, per M15 Batch 2/§40 — a documented, out-of-scope
  tooling limitation, not a new finding this session).
- **Quiz (6 questions):** analysis-phase task fit for AI; the correct fix for generic AI-drafted
  scenarios (grounding); assessment-integrity response to chatbot-completable tasks;
  UNESCO's actual emphasis; why alt-text/translation drafts still need human review; short-answer
  on why cheaper production doesn't automatically improve learning.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct; the workflow-mapping framing (good at compressing evidence, bad at
   inventing what learners need) is a clean, defensible distinction, not a vague generality.
2. **Clarity** — strong; "grounding beats instructing" is illustrated with a concrete
   before/after rather than asserted abstractly.
3. **Depth** — appropriate; explicitly cross-references Day 15's pattern rather than re-deriving

4. **Practicality** — high; the distractor-survival-rate exercise produces a personal

5. **Examples** — the grounded-scenario-writer example is specific and shows the mechanism

6. **Active learning** — yes: real distractor generation and scoring, a real assessment

7. **Misconceptions** — explicitly addressed: banning AI tools in response to a gameable

8. **Current relevance** — no gap found; the human-owned-source-of-truth pattern and the

9. **Resource quality** — the UNESCO resource remains appropriate and verified; its own

10. **Assessment alignment** — all three objectives directly tested (q1 → workflow-phase fit,

## Day 24 — AI, the LMS and Learning Data

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 7 — Applied AI for Learning. **Sub:** `lms`.
- **Objectives:** describe what data an LMS already produces and what it structurally cannot
  tell you; distinguish SCORM from xAPI by the question each can answer; decide where AI plugs
  into a learning stack without becoming the system of record.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, an LMS as an authoritative-but-narrow system of
  record; most "AI in the LMS" ideas fail because the needed data was never recorded) → Core
  material (20m, SCORM's small fixed vocabulary vs. xAPI's open actor-verb-object statements sent
  to a separate Learning Record Store, and the design cost that openness carries; where AI
  belongs in the stack — content generation, RAG-pattern assistants, assisted-not-authoritative
  scoring, LRS analytics — and where it does not — system of record, unreviewed at-risk labeling;
  privacy considerations) → Hands-on (15m, write three xAPI-shaped statements and the question
  each answers that a completion flag can't) → Practice (10m, instrument-before-you-model
  exercise) → Reflection (5m, one new tracked event and the decision it would change).
- **Example:** an at-risk flag that fires for everyone in week one because the only available
  signal (SCORM completion) is dominated by the calendar, not behaviour — and a callback noting
  this app's own opposite design choice (raw events, derived progress).
- **Practice/Challenge:** an instrumentation-first exercise; a minimum-instrumentation design
  task that explicitly asks the learner to then open this app's own Review system and judge
  whether it made the same three decisions well.
- **Resources (2, both `verified: true`):** xAPI.com's explainer (Rustici Software); the ADL
  Initiative's official xAPI-Spec repository, already flagged with a version-currency note from
  M15 Batch 2/§40 (repo holds v1.0.3; the IEEE-standardized v2.0 page could not itself be opened
  that session due to bot-blocking) — a documented, unchanged, out-of-scope item.
- **Quiz (6 questions):** the essential SCORM/xAPI difference; what a Learning Record Store is;
  why AI should not hold the official grade; the correct first move when only coarse completion
  data exists; what sending free-text learner answers to a hosted model actually means; a
  short-answer on why "add AI to our LMS" projects usually fail at the data layer.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct; the SCORM/xAPI distinction is precise (fixed vocabulary answering one
   question class vs. open vocabulary requiring the org to agree its own conventions), not a
   simplified "old vs. new" framing.
2. **Clarity** — strong; "instrument before you model" is stated as a named rule, not just
   implied by the example.
3. **Depth** — appropriate; correctly stops short of teaching xAPI statement syntax in detail,
   which would be premature here.
4. **Practicality** — high; the hands-on activity requires the learner to name the question each
   xAPI statement would answer, with an explicit "if you can't write that question, don't record
   the statement" filter — a genuinely transferable design habit, not busywork.
5. **Examples** — the at-risk-flag example is specific and correctly diagnoses the failure as an
   instrumentation problem, not a model-choice problem.
6. **Active learning** — yes: real statement-writing, an instrument-before-you-model worksheet,
   and a challenge that sends the learner back into this app's own Review system as a case study.
7. **Misconceptions** — explicitly addressed: "many 'AI in the LMS' ideas fail... because the
   model isn't smart enough" is named and corrected to "the data was never recorded."
8. **Current relevance** — no gap found; SCORM and xAPI are both long-stable standards (SCORM
   effectively frozen for years; xAPI's core statement shape unchanged since 1.0), so this is
   one of the least currency-sensitive days in the curriculum by design of the subject matter
   itself, not by omission.
9. **Resource quality** — both resources verified; the ADL-repo version-currency caveat from
   M15/§40 remains accurate and is already disclosed in the resource's own `hubungan` field —
   nothing new to add.
10. **Assessment alignment** — all three objectives directly tested (q1 → SCORM/xAPI distinction,
    q2 → LRS definition supporting the data objective, q3/q4 → where AI belongs and the
    correct-first-move diagnosis). No gap.

### Research Findings

No external research opened. SCORM and xAPI are stable, slow-moving standards; the one
