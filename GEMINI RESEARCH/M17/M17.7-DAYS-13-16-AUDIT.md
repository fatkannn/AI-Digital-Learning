## Day 14 — Deep Learning and Neural Networks

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 3 — AI Foundations. **Sub:** `dl`.
- **Objectives:** explain a neural network as layers of weighted transformations; describe what
  training adjusts and what inference does; explain why deep learning needs data and compute at
  scale.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, layers/weights/bias/non-linearity, "deep" = many
  layers) → Core material (20m, backpropagation/gradient descent, training-vs-inference cost
  asymmetry, architecture families — CNN/RNN/transformer — stated at the right level of
  abstraction: "you do not need the maths yet") → Hands-on (15m, browser neural-network
  playground, vary layers/neurons/learning rate) → Practice (10m, explain a too-high learning
  rate's effect) → Reflection (5m, "learned" vs. "understands").
- **Example:** local inference (a forward pass over frozen weights) is realistic; local training
  is not — a clean, correctly-reasoned distinction that prevents unrealistic project planning.
- **Resources (2):** Google ML Crash Course's deep-learning module (`verified: true`), Jay
  Alammar's "The Illustrated Transformer" (`verified: true`) — a well-regarded, still-current
  visual explainer of the transformer architecture.
- **Quiz (6 questions):** training-vs-inference distinction, "more layers is always better"
  (false), activation-function purpose, objection to training an LLM from scratch, which
  architecture underpins modern LLMs, short-answer on local-inference feasibility.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct; backpropagation, the training/inference cost asymmetry, and the
   architecture-family overview all hold up.
2. **Clarity** — strong; deliberately avoids equations, consistent with its own stated design
   ("you do not need the maths yet").
3. **Depth** — appropriate; correctly stops short of attention-mechanism mathematics, which
   belongs to a later, more specialised treatment if it existed at all in this curriculum.
4. **Practicality** — high; the neural-network playground hands-on activity is genuinely
   interactive, not passive reading.
5. **Examples** — the local-inference-vs-local-training example is accurate and load-bearing for
   later days (it sets up realistic expectations before Ollama/local-model material).
6. **Active learning** — yes.
7. **Misconceptions** — two are explicitly named: "deep" ≠ deeper understanding, and more layers
   is not automatically better.
8. **Current relevance** — considered whether newer, non-transformer architectures (e.g.
   state-space models) deserve a mention; judged out of scope for a Day 14 beginner lesson whose
   own design principle is "you do not need the maths yet" — introducing a second architecture
   family here would add jargon without adding capability at this point in the curriculum. Not a
   gap; a correct scoping decision.
9. **Resource quality** — both resources solid, verified, and well-matched to the day's depth.
10. **Assessment alignment** — all three objectives are directly tested (layer/weight structure
    via the activation-function question, training-vs-inference via two questions, why deep
    learning needs scale via the "train from scratch" scenario question).

## Day 15 — Generative AI

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 3 — AI Foundations. **Sub:** `genai`.
- **Objectives:** explain what makes a model generative rather than discriminative; describe the
  main modalities and their typical failure modes; judge where generative AI genuinely helps in
  learning content production.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, discriminative vs. generative, plausibility ≠
  correctness) → Core material (20m, next-token prediction / noise-reversal as the same
  sampling principle, hallucination as a structural property not a bug, the human-owned-source-
  of-truth production pattern) → Hands-on (15m, generate 5 MCQs from supplied text, audit each
  distractor) → Practice (10m, fix the two weakest questions via prompt constraints) →
  Reflection (5m, where generation saves real time vs. where review costs more than writing).
- **Example:** "generation is drafting, not authoring" — 200 quiz questions generated in an
  hour, three days spent finding a fifth were subtly wrong; the honest metric is time-to-
  publishable, not time-to-draft.
- **Resources (2):** Microsoft's "Generative AI for Beginners" (`verified: true`), Hugging Face
  Learn (`verified: true`).
- **Quiz (6 questions):** generative-vs-discriminative definition, fluency-is-not-correctness,
  lowest-risk use of generative AI in course production, honest efficiency metric, modality
  identification, short-answer on why models hallucinate confidently.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct throughout; the discriminative/generative distinction, the "same
   sampling principle, different mechanism" framing for text vs. image generation, and the
   hallucination explanation are all standard and correctly stated.
2. **Clarity** — strong; avoids naming specific products or model families, which keeps the
   explanation from aging against any one vendor's release cycle.
3. **Depth** — appropriate.
4. **Practicality** — high; the MCQ-generation-and-audit hands-on activity is the single most
   directly transferable exercise so far in the curriculum for this app's own target audience
   (instructional designers).
5. **Examples** — the "generation is drafting, not authoring" example is concrete and
   quantified, not hand-wavy.
6. **Active learning** — yes.
7. **Misconceptions** — hallucination-as-bug-that-will-be-fixed is explicitly and correctly
   rejected.
8. **Current relevance** — considered whether specific modern generative modalities (e.g. video
   generation, voice cloning) warrant a named mention; judged unnecessary — the lesson's own
   modality list is already deliberately generic ("text, images, audio, video, code") and naming
   specific products would date the lesson for no pedagogical gain, since the underlying failure
   mode (plausible ≠ correct) is the actual teaching point and is modality-agnostic.
9. **Resource quality** — both resources solid, verified, and appropriately scoped (Microsoft's

   referenced to Day 16 embeddings and local AI).
10. **Assessment alignment** — all three objectives directly tested (generative-vs-discriminative
    definition, modality/hallucination question, and two questions specifically on applied
    judgement in content production — the lowest-risk-use scenario and the honest-metric

## Day 16 — LLM Fundamentals: Tokens and Context Window

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 4 — LLM Foundations. **Sub:** `llm`.
- **Objectives:** explain tokenisation and its effect on cost and limits; describe what the
  context window holds and why it is finite; diagnose problems caused by context loss or
  truncation.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, tokens as the model's actual input unit, context
  window as everything competing for space — instructions, history, retrieved documents, tool
  results, the response itself) → Core material (20m, tokenisation cost/pricing implications,
  window-exceeded truncation, positional reliability within a long context) → Hands-on (15m,
  compare English-vs-Indonesian token counts in a tokeniser, compare table/code density) →
  Practice (10m, estimate token cost and monthly cost at 200 runs) → Reflection (5m, why "paste
  the whole handbook" is a bad default on both cost and quality grounds).
- **Example:** "the assistant that forgot the rules" — durable instructions placed in a chat
  message age out of the context window; the architectural fix is the system prompt, which is
  re-sent every turn.
- **Resources (2):** Hugging Face's LLM Course (`verified: true` — its precision was already
  independently reviewed and resolved as "keep current URL" in M16.2/§44, not a new question
  here), Google's Gemini API documentation (`verified: true`).
- **Quiz (6 questions):** token definition, context-window contents, truncation diagnosis,
  why tokenisation cost differs by language, why retrieval beats pasting an entire document,
  short-answer on what token count affects.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct throughout, including the language-cost-asymmetry claim (a tokeniser
   trained predominantly on English text represents English word-pieces more efficiently) and
   the positional-reliability claim.
2. **Clarity** — strong; the "assistant that forgot the rules" example makes truncation concrete
   and ties directly into the system-instructions architecture point the curriculum revisits on
   Day 17.
3. **Depth** — appropriate.
4. **Practicality** — high; the token-cost-at-scale practice exercise (per-call estimate ×
   monthly volume) is the kind of calculation this app's target learners will actually need to
   do.
5. **Examples** — accurate and well-chosen; deliberately avoids citing a specific model's exact
   context-window size as a hard fact (the "200k-token window" in the challenge is used
   explicitly as an illustrative hypothetical, not asserted as a specific current model's real
   limit), which is a correct design choice that avoids the lesson going stale as window sizes
   change.
6. **Active learning** — yes.
7. **Misconceptions** — "the model forgot" is explicitly reframed as truncation, a deliberate
   and correct engineering fact rather than a memory failure.
8. **Current relevance — one finding, researched and confirmed, see below.**
9. **Resource quality** — both resources solid and verified.
10. **Assessment alignment** — all three objectives are directly tested (tokenisation/cost via
    two questions, context-window contents via one question, diagnosing truncation via one
    scenario question, plus the short-answer question reinforcing token-count effects broadly).

### Research Findings

The Core material's positional-reliability claim — "information buried in the middle of a very
long context is used less reliably than material at the start or end" — is accurate and, on
verification, **still current**, but the lesson does not name the phenomenon. Opened directly
(not from a search snippet):

- **Anthropic, "Context windows"** (`https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/context-windows`,
  official, current documentation page). **FACT**, quoted directly: *"As token count grows,
  accuracy and recall degrade, a phenomenon known as context rot."* This corroborates the
  lesson's existing claim — it does not correct anything, and the underlying content was already
  accurate — but names, with current official terminology, the exact pattern the lesson already
  describes informally.

**Source quality: A — official/primary.**

**INFERENCE:** a learner who later encounters "context rot" in real AI documentation or industry
discussion has no link back to a concept they already know, since the lesson currently teaches
the effect without naming it.

**RECOMMENDATION:** add the term to the existing sentence, without expanding the explanation —
the underlying content needs no correction, only a label.

### Decision

**ENHANCE**

### Priority

**P2** — useful, not urgent; the existing explanation is complete and correct on its own. This
is a vocabulary-precision addition, not a new concept, and is smaller in scope than M17.2's Day 1
NPU addition (which introduced a previously-absent concept). Cleared all 10 conditions in the
autonomous-implementation checklist — see `M17-IMPLEMENTATION-LOG.md` for the full accounting.

### Evidence

One official Anthropic source (quality A), quoted above, opened directly this session.

### Proposed Enhancement

Append the term "context rot" to the existing "position matters" sentence in Core material,
attributed to Anthropic, without adding a new sentence, a new segment, a new recap glossary
term, or a new quiz question.

### Implementation

**IMPLEMENTED.** Exact wording: the sentence "Position matters too: information buried in the
middle of a very long context is used less reliably than material at the start or end. This is
a practical argument for..." became "Position matters too: information buried in the middle of
a very long context is used less reliably than material at the start or end — part of a broader
pattern Anthropic calls context rot, where accuracy and recall quietly degrade as token count
grows, position aside. This is a practical argument for..." — one clause inserted, nothing
removed, nothing else in the day touched.

### Time Impact

Negligible — under 15 seconds of additional reading; no new timed segment, no change to the
day's 60-minute total.

### Assessment Impact

None. The existing context-window and truncation-diagnosis questions remain valid and complete
without a term-recognition item; a P2 vocabulary enrichment does not need its own quiz question,
consistent with the precedent set by M17.2's Day 1 enhancement.

### Resource Impact

None. No new resource added; the two existing resources remain unchanged and sufficient.
