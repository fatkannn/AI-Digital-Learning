## Day 25 — Cloud Computing

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 8 — Infrastructure and Security. **Sub:** `cloud`.
- **Objectives:** state what makes something cloud rather than ordinary hosting; place IaaS,
  PaaS and SaaS on a spectrum of who manages what; judge when cloud is the wrong answer on cost,
  control or data-residency grounds.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, NIST's five defining characteristics, with elasticity
  and measured service singled out as the two that change *design*, not just hosting location) →
  Core material (20m, the IaaS/PaaS/SaaS responsibility spectrum with managed services in
  between; shared responsibility and the customer-side-misconfiguration reality; three cost traps
  — idle resources, egress, per-token AI metering; why AI inference is a genuinely cloud-shaped
  problem; region as a legal, not just technical, decision) → Hands-on (15m, classify three
  real services and assign responsibility for four tasks) → Practice (10m, sketch a monthly cost
  breakdown for a small AI study assistant) → Reflection (5m, which characteristic matters least
  for your own workload).
- **Example:** an egress-dominated cloud bill correctly diagnosed and fixed with a caching layer,
  not a cheaper storage tier.
- **Resources (2, both `verified: true`):** NIST SP 800-145 (the canonical cloud definition,
  unchanged since 2011); AWS's IaaS/PaaS/SaaS explainer, explicitly framed in its own summary as
  vendor content to read with that lens.
- **Quiz (6 questions):** what distinguishes cloud from rented hosting; identifying PaaS from a
  description; shared-responsibility misconception; diagnosing an egress-dominated bill; why
  region is a legal decision; short-answer on why pay-as-you-go isn't automatically cheaper.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct; the NIST five-characteristics framing and the responsibility-spectrum
   model both match the standard, non-marketing definition of cloud computing.
2. **Clarity** — strong; "elasticity" and "measured service" are explained by their *design*
   consequence, not just defined.
3. **Depth** — appropriate; correctly stops at conceptual understanding rather than teaching a
   specific provider's console.
4. **Practicality** — high; the responsibility-matrix hands-on task (who patches, who backs up,
   who sets the password, who encrypts) produces genuinely uncertain rows the learner has to
   go look up, which is the actual point.
5. **Examples** — the egress-bill example is specific and models the correct diagnostic move
   (movement, not storage, is usually the surprise line item).
6. **Active learning** — yes: real-service classification, a cost sketch with an explicit
   fixed/per-use/unknown breakdown, and a challenge that separates the technical and legal
   questions in a real "should we move to cloud" decision.
7. **Misconceptions** — explicitly addressed: pay-per-use is not automatically cheaper; moving to
   cloud does not move away all responsibility.
8. **Current relevance** — no gap found; see Research Findings.
9. **Resource quality** — both resources verified; the AWS resource is honestly labeled as
   vendor-authored rather than presented as neutral.
10. **Assessment alignment** — all three objectives directly tested (q1 → cloud-vs-hosting
    definition, q2 → service-model spectrum, q4/q5 → the two "when cloud is wrong" grounds named
    in objective 3: cost and residency; control is discussed in Core material and implicit in
    q3's shared-responsibility question). No gap.

own resource summary already states as a feature ("terbit 2011 dan tidak berubah sejak itu —
yang justru menandakan definisinya bertahan"), and the responsibility-spectrum/cost-trap content
is conceptual rather than tied to a specific provider's current pricing or product lineup. No
candidate gap was identified worth spending a fetch on.

## Day 26 — Cybersecurity Fundamentals

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 8 — Infrastructure and Security. **Sub:** `security`.
- **Objectives:** use confidentiality, integrity and availability to state precisely what is
  being protected; distinguish authentication from authorisation and explain why MFA outranks
  password complexity; **read a vendor or a system you built against a recognised list of common
  risks.**
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, the CIA triad, and naming which of the three is at
  stake as a way to end vague security conversations) → Core material (20m, authn vs. authz and
  why broken access control outranks injection on the OWASP web list; credential reuse as the
  real danger via credential stuffing, length over complexity, MFA as the highest-value control;
  phishing after generative fluency killed grammar as a signal; encryption in transit vs. at
  rest; patching and supply chain; the 3-2-1 backup rule) → Hands-on (15m, a real self-audit of
  MFA status and password reuse) → Practice (10m, a read/change/notify access matrix for a real
  system) → Reflection (5m, what would a stolen, encrypted laptop actually cost you).
- **Example:** a compromise with no software vulnerability at all — reused password, no MFA,
  over-broad role — correctly framing security work as "mostly boring, and the boring parts are
  the ones that pay."
- **Practice/Challenge:** a real access-control audit; **a ten-question vendor-evaluation
  exercise explicitly built from the CIA triad and "the common risk categories"** — this is

  Impact).
- **Resources (2, both `verified: true`, both re-verified with direct content checks in M15

- **Quiz (6 questions):** CIA-triad property identification from a scenario; authn/authz failure
  diagnosis; MFA-vs-password-complexity misconception; phishing response under generative-fluency
  conditions; what encryption at rest actually protects against; short-answer on why backups beat
  detection alone.

### Pedagogical Audit

4. **Practicality** — very high; the MFA self-audit and password-reuse count are real, immediate,

## Day 27 — Workflow Automation and n8n

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 9 — Automation and Integration. **Sub:** `automation`.
- **Objectives:** describe an automation in terms of trigger, nodes, data and credentials;
  decide whether a task is worth automating using frequency, time saved and error cost; design a
  workflow that fails loudly and safely rather than silently.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, the small, reusable automation vocabulary; why n8n
  specifically — visual, self-hostable, shows the data at every step) → Core material (20m,
  schedule vs. webhook vs. app-event triggers and their tradeoffs; data reshaping as most of the
  real work, tying back to Day 10's JSON skills; the four failure-design requirements — retries,
  idempotency, an error branch, human notification; credentials per Day 22's rule; AI nodes as
  probabilistic steps inside a deterministic structure; the automate-when decision rule) →
  Hands-on (15m, map a real manual process as trigger→steps→outputs) → Practice (10m, add
  failure design to the mapped process and check its idempotency) → Reflection (5m, which step
  did you assume never fails).
- **Example:** a double-enrollment/double-email incident correctly diagnosed as a missing
  idempotency check, with the fix stated precisely (check-before-create, retry the failed step
  not the whole run).
- **Resources (2, both `verified: true`, the n8n glossary directly re-verified with a full
  content check in M15 Batch 3/§41 and found to be broader/richer than its old summary, which was
  corrected at that time):** n8n's official "Build your first workflow" tutorial; n8n's official
  key-concept glossary.
- **Quiz (6 questions):** the role of a trigger node; the practical advantage of webhook over
  schedule triggers; why a twice-yearly task is a poor automation candidate; diagnosing a missing
  idempotency check from a double-enrollment symptom; why silent failure is worse than no
  automation; short-answer on why a dependable workflow must notify on failure.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — correct; the trigger-type tradeoffs, the idempotency framing, and the
   automate-when decision rule (frequency × time saved vs. build + maintenance cost) are all
   standard, sound automation-engineering principles.
2. **Clarity** — strong; "data reshaping is most of the real work" is a genuinely useful
   reframing for a beginner who might assume automation is mostly about calling APIs.
3. **Depth** — appropriate; deliberately stays at the trigger/failure-design/decision-rule level
   rather than teaching n8n's UI in detail.
4. **Practicality** — high; the map-a-real-process exercise uses the learner's actual work, not
   an invented scenario.
5. **Examples** — the double-enrollment example is specific and the fix is stated with the right
   level of precision (retry the step, not the run).
6. **Active learning** — yes: real-process mapping, failure-design addition, and an idempotency
   self-check ("what happens if this runs twice on the same input").
7. **Misconceptions** — explicitly addressed: a silently-failing automation is worse than none,

8. **Current relevance** — no gap found; the vocabulary and failure-design principles taught are
   tool-agnostic and stable, and the two resources were already freshly re-verified.
9. **Resource quality** — both resources verified, and the glossary's summary was already

10. **Assessment alignment** — objectives 2 and 3 are directly and cleanly tested (q3 → automate-

## Day 28 — MCP: The Model Context Protocol

### Current Curriculum

- **Phase:** 9 — Automation and Integration. **Sub:** `mcp`.
- **Objectives:** state the problem MCP solves and why it is an integration problem, not a model
  problem; name the roles — host, client, server — and the three capabilities a server can
  expose; assess what you are accepting when you install a third-party MCP server.
- **Lesson structure:** Concept intro (10m, the mechanism is unchanged from Day 20; what changes
  is who writes the connector — the N×M integration problem MCP solves) → Core material (20m,
  the three roles; the three server capabilities — resources, prompts, tools — plus client
  capabilities including sampling and elicitation; the reuse argument for treating this as a
  protocol rather than bespoke tool calls; a direct, unsoftened security section quoting the
  spec's own language; an honest maturity/versioning caveat) → Hands-on (15m, audit every MCP
  server actually connected to a tool the learner uses, and what each can read/do) → Practice
  (10m, design a three-tool MCP server and mark which tool would not get write access) →
  Reflection (5m, which of your own tool descriptions would a model misuse).
- **Example:** a full-scope API token issued to a community MCP server — correctly framed as
  Day 26's ordinary dependency risk with a live credential attached, not an exotic new threat.
- **Practice/Challenge:** a three-tool MCP server design exercise; a capstone comparing tool
  calling (Day 20), workflow automation (Day 27) and MCP (today), forcing the learner to name the
  one fact that decides between them for a real problem.
- **Resources (2, both `verified: true`):** the official MCP Specification (pointing at the
  2025-06-18 revision, with the resource's own summary already disclosing a newer 2026-07-28
  revision exists and instructing the reader to check current status); Anthropic's original MCP
  announcement (explicitly labeled as November 2024 and "read for the problem, not current
  status").
- **Quiz (6 questions):** the N×M integration problem MCP solves; the three server capabilities;
  the "open standard = inherently safer" misconception, corrected directly against the spec's own
  security language; the correct response to a full-scope-token setup prompt; identifying the
  host role; short-answer on what MCP standardises and why reuse matters.

### Pedagogical Audit

1. **Accuracy** — **directly confirmed this session, see Research Findings.** Correct throughout.
2. **Clarity** — strong; the security section is deliberately blunt rather than softened, which
   is the right register for a "you are running a stranger's code" warning.
3. **Depth** — appropriate; correctly distinguishes the mechanism (unchanged since Day 20) from
   what's actually new (who writes the connector), avoiding re-teaching tool calling from
   scratch.
4. **Practicality** — high; the hands-on task (audit every MCP server actually connected to a
   tool you use, and what it can read/do) is a real, personally applicable security exercise, not
   a hypothetical.
5. **Examples** — the full-scope-token example is specific and its fix (a read-only token that
   costs nothing) is concrete and actionable.
6. **Active learning** — yes: a real connected-server audit, a three-tool design exercise with an
   explicit write-access decision, and a capstone that forces a real choice among three
   mechanisms taught across the curriculum.
7. **Misconceptions** — explicitly and strongly addressed: "MCP does not make tool use safer by
   itself" is stated directly, quoting the spec's own security principles rather than softening
   them.
8. **Current relevance** — **confirmed this session**, see Research Findings; the lesson already
   honestly discloses its own resource's version-lag rather than presenting a moving target as
   settled.
9. **Resource quality** — both resources verified and appropriately captioned with their own
   currency limitations, an unusually well-disclosed pair.
10. **Assessment alignment** — objectives 1 and 3 are directly and cleanly tested (q1 →
    integration-problem framing, q4 → third-party-server risk assessment). Objective 2 ("name the
    roles... and the three capabilities") is tested for capabilities (q2, clean hit) and for one
    role, host (q5); client and server are distinguished in q5's explanation but not independently
    quizzed. Weighed the same way as Day 27's vocabulary question and judged the same way: this is
    a shared-vocabulary objective substantially covered by q2 and q5 together, not a clean zero
    like Day 26's objective 3 — not flagged, to avoid the same over-flagging risk.

### Research Findings

Given how fast MCP specifically evolves (the lesson's own resource already flags a newer revision
exists), and that this is the single fastest-moving topic in the entire 28-day curriculum, this
was judged worth a direct check — not of a resource link, but of whether the lesson's *own
teaching* (not just its citation) still matches the spec.

Opened directly:

- **Model Context Protocol, official specification (2025-06-18 revision — the same page already
  cited as this day's own resource)** (`https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18`,
  official primary source, quality **A**). **FACT, confirmed line-by-line against the lesson:**
  the three roles (host/client/server) match exactly, including the definitions used; the three
  server capabilities (resources/prompts/tools) match exactly, including their descriptions
  ("context and data," "templated messages and workflows," "functions... to execute"); JSON-RPC
  as the base protocol is confirmed; client-offered capabilities include sampling and elicitation
  as the lesson states (the spec also lists a third, "roots," which the lesson does not mention —
  an omission, not an error, and not flagged as a gap since the lesson's own stated scope is the
  three it names, not an exhaustive capability list); the security section's specific claims —
  "tools represent arbitrary code execution," tool-behavior descriptions "should be considered
  untrusted... unless obtained from a trusted server," and mandatory "explicit user consent
  before invoking any tool" — all match the spec's actual wording closely enough to confirm the
  lesson is paraphrasing accurately, not inventing or softening it.

**RECOMMENDATION (not applied):** none. This check produced a **confirmation**, not a finding —
the lesson's core teaching is accurate against the primary source, on the same page this day's
own resource already points to. Recorded in full because a fast-moving topic having been checked
and found correct is itself worth documenting, the same way M17.9 documented a check that came
back inconclusive-but-not-contradicted for Day 22 — the discipline is to record what was actually
verified either way, not only when something needs to change.
